How to Read Surf Conditions

Surf reports are full of numbers — wave height, period, wind, tides. Here's what each one actually means, and how they come together to tell you whether it's worth paddling out.

The surf score, in one line

Chicken Joe condenses everything into a single surf score from 0 to 10. It's a quick gut-check: higher is better. But the score is only a starting point — understanding the individual ingredients below will make you a far better forecaster than any single number.

Wave height

This is the size of the waves, usually measured in metres (or feet). It's the first thing most people look at, but bigger is not always better. A clean, well-organised one-metre swell can be far more fun and surfable than a messy, wind-blown two-metre mess.

Rough guide

HeightWho it suits
0.3–0.6 mBeginners, longboarders, small-wave days
0.6–1.2 mMost surfers — the sweet spot for fun
1.2–2 mConfident intermediates and up
2 m+Experienced surfers only

Swell period — the secret ingredient

The period is the time in seconds between each wave. It's arguably more important than height, and the number most beginners ignore.

  • Short period (under 8 seconds): usually local wind chop. Weak, disorganised, closes out quickly.
  • Medium period (8–12 seconds): decent groundswell. Waves have more push and form better.
  • Long period (13+ seconds): powerful groundswell that has travelled a long way. Even a small long-period swell can produce punchy, well-shaped waves.

Two days can both show "1 metre" but feel completely different. A 1 m / 14 s day will have real power; a 1 m / 6 s day will be soft and gutless. Period is why.

Wind — onshore vs offshore

Wind direction makes or breaks a session. It's the single biggest factor in whether waves are clean or messy.

  • Offshore wind blows from the land out to sea. It holds the wave face up, grooming it clean and creating those glassy, hollow waves you see in photos. This is what you want.
  • Onshore wind blows from the sea toward land. It crumbles waves from behind, making them mushy, bumpy and hard to ride.
  • Cross-shore wind blows along the beach — somewhere in between, usually choppy.

Light winds (under ~10 km/h) of any direction are generally fine. The stronger the wind, the more its direction matters. This is also why dawn sessions are prized: wind is usually lightest in the early morning.

Tides

The rise and fall of the ocean changes how waves break over the sand or reef below. Some spots work best at low tide, others at high, and many on the "push" or "drop" in between. There's no universal rule — it's spot-specific — but knowing the tide times lets you plan around them. Watching how a break behaves across the tide is one of the fastest ways to learn a new spot.

Reading the 7-day forecast

A forecast chart shows how conditions will evolve. The skill is in spotting the windows: a rising swell combined with light morning winds and a favourable tide is the combination you're hunting for. Don't just look at the biggest day — look for the cleanest one.

Surf etiquette & safety

  • Right of way: the surfer closest to the breaking part of the wave (the peak) has priority. Don't drop in on them.
  • Don't paddle straight through the line-up — go around the breaking waves.
  • Know your limits. Check the wave height honestly and respect rips, rocks and crowds.
  • Never surf alone in unfamiliar or large conditions, and always check local hazard signage.
